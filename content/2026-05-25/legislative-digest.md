- **Why it matters:** SB 26-189 cleared the Senate on 7 May and the House on 9 May; Governor Polis signed on 14 May. Weiser ties the PI motion clock to any amending legislation; that clock now runs to ~11 June. The federal preemption playbook is therefore now litigating against a state law that no longer exists in its original form — a procedural posture more favourable to xAI/DOJ than to Colorado. SaferAI read: the federal-state preemption test SaferAI flagged across the past four digests is no longer a contest of whether state safety statutes survive — it is a contest about whether *anything* in this space survives without being framed as light-touch transparency. California SB 53's incident-reporting framework (Cal OES, in force since 1 January 2026) is now the next plausible federal target.

### AISI publishes sycophancy-reduction paper — input-phrasing intervention 🆕 NEW
- **Organiser:** UK AI Security Institute (DSIT)
- **Jurisdiction:** UK
- **Stage:** published (policy input)
- **Deadline:** n/a (published 2026-04-29; in scope for the past-14-day delta as second-pass uptake landed in early May)
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/ask-dont-tell-reducing-sycophancy-in-large-language-models-2
  - https://www.resultsense.com/news/2026-04-29-aisi-sycophancy-research-llms
  - https://arxiv.org/abs/2602.23971
- **Why it matters:** AISI's Human Influence team published "Ask Don't Tell" examining how user-input phrasing modulates sycophantic behaviour in deployed-class frontier models (GPT-5, Claude family). Headline findings: questions produce near-zero sycophancy; non-question inputs expressing the same claim produce up to 24 percentage points more flattery; epistemic certainty ("I am convinced…") and first-person framing amplify the effect; a simple system-prompt rewrite ("rephrase the user's input as a question before responding") cuts sycophancy more reliably than instructing the model not to be sycophantic. Less directly policy-consequential than the 28 April sabotage paper, but methodologically important because it gives supervisors a measurable behavioural lever that does not depend on training-time intervention — useful evidence for the AI Office's downstream-deployer obligations work and for Article 50 deployer-disclosure guidance.

## 💡 EDITORIAL NOTE

The structural read is that the AI Act has crossed from political negotiation into operational implementation while two of SaferAI's neighbouring jurisdictions have moved in the opposite direction. In Brussels, three consultations are simultaneously running — Article 50 (closes 3 June), high-risk classification (closes 23 June), and the residual energy-emissions methodology track behind us — and CADA reaches College on 27 May, all under a Cyprus Presidency that has fewer than five weeks left. The EU's 15 May ratification of the CoE Framework Convention adds a treaty-level human-rights anchor under the Act's downstream-deployer obligations exactly as the carve-outs negotiated in the Omnibus narrow them in domestic law. In London, the King's Speech declined the AI Bill in favour of Henry-VIII sandbox powers; in Denver, SB 26-189 has formally emptied the statute that was the test case for federal preemption, leaving xAI/DOJ to litigate ~11 June against a regime that is not yet in force. For SaferAI the operational priorities are clear: (i) Article 50 submission before 3 June, with explicit reference to the deployer-disclosure interface with Article 92 model-access powers; (ii) high-risk classification submission before 23 June, focusing on Annex III items 1, 5 and 8; (iii) CADA position briefed into the EVP cabinet before 27 May, anchored on safe-deployment conditionality rather than narrow market-access framing; (iv) a Dublin read on Irish-Presidency continuity by mid-June; (v) tracking the next federal-preemption target — California SB 53's incident-reporting framework is now the obvious next move.
