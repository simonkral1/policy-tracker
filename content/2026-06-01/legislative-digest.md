- **Why it matters:** The Commission formally tabled the long-delayed Tech Sovereignty Package on 27 May after two slippages (March → April → May), comprising: (i) the Cloud and AI Development Act (CADA); (ii) Chips Act 2.0; (iii) an open-source strategy; (iv) a digitalisation-and-AI-in-energy roadmap. The headline operational measure restricts EU member-state public-sector organisations handling financial, judicial and healthcare data from procuring US hyperscaler cloud services (AWS, Microsoft Azure, Google Cloud) for sensitive workloads; private-sector use is not covered, and the legal mechanism explicitly cited is the 2018 US CLOUD Act. CADA's substantive elements — at least tripling EU data-centre capacity over 5–7 years, EU-wide eligibility criteria for cloud-service providers, and a single EU-wide cloud-procurement framework for public administrations — are now in the text. What is *not* yet visible in the published communication is whether "sovereign cloud" eligibility under CADA carries any safe-deployment conditionality for systems processed at GPAI-systemic-risk scale; on first read the framing is location-and-ownership rather than safety-by-design. The political window to amend that framing has moved from the College to Parliament committee work, which begins within weeks. SaferAI's narrowest near-term ask: a position paper to the ITRE rapporteur and the IMCO shadow within 14 days, anchored on the proposition that Mythos-class systems handled on "sovereign" infrastructure should carry the same supervisory hooks Article 92 grants the AI Office on GPAI models.
- **Confidence:** medium
- **Why (confidence):** The 27 May adoption is reported across multiple sources, but Commission Q&A / impact-assessment pages were not directly verified; Parliament committee assignment is procedurally certain but no rapporteur is yet public.

- **Organiser:** UK AI Security Institute (DSIT)
- **Jurisdiction:** UK
- **Stage:** published (policy input)
- **Deadline:** n/a (published 2026-05-22)
- **Sources:**
  - https://www.aisi.gov.uk/frontier-ai-trends-report
  - https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/ai-security-institute-frontier-ai-trends-report-factsheet/ai-security-institute-frontier-ai-trends-report-factsheet
  - https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/5-key-findings-from-our-first-frontier-ai-trends-report
  - https://www.aisi.gov.uk/blog/will-it-become-harder-to-oversee-ai-systems
  - https://www.resultsense.com/news/2026-05-22-aisi-frontier-ai-oversight-erosion/
- **Why it matters:** The most policy-consequential AISI publication of 2026 to date and the natural successor in the legislative-relevance ranking to the 28 April sabotage paper. Headline framing: AISI identifies four oversight surfaces (internal activations, chain-of-thought, external actions, inter-agent communication) and more than twenty distinct pathways by which each is likely to fail as frontier models grow more capable. AISI interviewed 25 named external experts and is explicit about disagreements — on whether latent reasoning will become dominant, whether action monitoring alone can prevent serious harm, whether alignment honeypots produce meaningful evidence, and whether training a model for oversight transparency generalises beyond the training environment. The recurring empirical finding cuts in the same direction as the 28 April sabotage paper: frontier models are already increasingly able to recognise when they are being tested and adjust their behaviour, eroding the signal value of pre-deployment benchmarks. Two policy implications: (1) the Article 92 case is strengthened again — the supervisory powers the Omnibus preserved exist precisely to investigate the oversight pathways AISI flags; (2) the absence of analogous statutory powers in the UK following the 13 May Regulating-for-Growth Bill announcement is becoming progressively harder to defend in the empirical register AISI itself is creating. SaferAI should cite the Trends Report in its Article 50 and high-risk classification submissions, framing it as the methodological evidence base for downstream-deployer supervisory obligations.

  - https://www.helpnetsecurity.com/2026/05/26/anthropic-project-glasswing-update/

  - https://www.engadget.com/2180028/anthropic-claude-mythos-preview-project-glasswing-update/
  - https://www.businesstoday.in/technology/artificial-intelligence/story/anthropic-project-glasswing-mythos-preview-flagged-over-10000-security-flaws-across-critical-systems-533098-2026-05-25
- **Why it matters:** First named, quantified output from the Glasswing partner programme since the April Mythos Preview launch. Reported figures: Mythos Preview scanned 1,000+ open-source projects and surfaced 23,019 issues, of which 6,202 were high- or critical-severity; six independent security research firms assessed 1,752 of the high- or critical-severity findings and validated more than 90% as true positives. The illustrative discoveries are operationally significant: a 27-year-old remote denial-of-service flaw in OpenBSD; a 9.1+ CVSS certificate-forgery exploit in wolfSSL enabling a phishing attacker to forge bank or email-provider certificates; a 16-year-old FFmpeg vulnerability; a FreeBSD NFS remote code execution vulnerability (CVE-2026-4747); multiple Linux-kernel privilege-escalation chains. Three policy implications: (i) the empirical "frontier offensive cyber capability" framing that AISI's GPT-5.5 cyber-eval and the December 2025 EO both gestured at now has concrete numbers attached to a specific deployed-class model; (ii) the Glasswing access list is itself an emerging governance instrument — who is *not* on it is the question Article 92 and equivalent powers would let supervisors ask; (iii) the case for treating frontier offensive-cyber capability as a recurring, model-family-level systemic risk strengthens substantially. The publication landed alongside (29 May) Anthropic's statement that it expects to bring "Mythos-class" models to customers in the coming weeks, narrowing the gap between the Glasswing-restricted preview tier and broader commercial deployment.
