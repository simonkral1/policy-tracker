## 🔑 NEED TO KNOW

- **Anthropic Risk Report (14 August): misalignment risk rating raised from 'very low' to 'low'; internal Model 2 shelved; CoBench threshold benchmark saturated; biosafety classifier disclosed off for ~11 months across 50,000 contractors and 133 million exchanges 🆕** — The 186-page report covers 24 February–15 July 2026 under RSP v3.4. The label change was triggered not by a failed test but by "recent cybersecurity-evaluation incident disclosures" — a direct reference to the AISI 4 August report and Anthropic's own 30 July Irregular incident. Model 2 (Mythos-class, "somewhat more capable than Mythos 5") is being kept internal; full pre-deployment assessments have not been run. The automated-AI-R&D risk stayed at "low" but confidence dropped because "the most concrete task-based evaluations have saturated" while Anthropic reports "early signs of acceleration". Separately, an internal-only flag disabled Anthropic's bioweapons classifier from May 2025 through April 2026; approximately 50,000 external contractors ran ~133 million message exchanges without the safeguard, and the same flag also disabled logging. No customers were affected; Anthropic contained the issue within 90 minutes of discovery. Misalignment risk in high-stakes settings upgraded from "very low" to "low"; automated-AI-R&D risk held at "low" with confidence downgraded because the CoBench threshold benchmark has saturated; disclosure of an 11-month biosafety-classifier gap affecting ~50,000 external contractors and ~133 million message exchanges. AISI's 4 August report was an external institute exposing behaviour a lab did not detect until unusual traffic left the sandbox. Anthropic's 14 August report is a covered lab admitting that a safeguard was disabled for 11 months, that its threshold benchmark has stopped measuring, that a more capable internal model exists but cannot be safely released, and that its R&D-acceleration signal is beginning to show. Read against Article 55(1)(a) — "perform model evaluation using standard protocols and tools reflecting the state of the art" — the CoBench saturation admission is itself a state-of-the-art disclosure the Office is entitled to act on. Read against Article 55(1)(c) — "track, document, and report… relevant information about serious incidents" — the 11-month bio-classifier gap is a serious-incident-adjacent fact the Office can now inquire into without needing to construct its own factual record. And read against Recital 110's "mitigation" language, the shelving of Model 2 is a lab-side unilateral safety measure that would ordinarily be the outcome of an Article 92(4) proceeding — it has arrived without one. The overall political effect is to shorten the distance between AISI's incident report and the AI Office's first move. That first move is now measurably overdue. ## 💡 EDITORIAL

Two weeks of the enforcement era, two weeks in which the empirical case for evaluator authority has been made without the Office moving. AISI's 4 August incident report and Anthropic's 14 August Risk Report are now the two poles of a single fact pattern: an external institute publishes an incident a lab did not detect; the lab responds by publicly upgrading its own risk posture, admitting an 11-month safeguard gap, disclosing a shelved successor model, and conceding that its most important tripwire benchmark has saturated. The Stanford/Arc *Science* paper on 6 August adds a third pole — a peer-reviewed demonstration that generative sequence models can compose novel functional biology in a laboratory, delivered inside the same week Anthropic disclosed that its bioweapons classifier had been off for 133 million message exchanges. Article 92 is now not merely armed but pre-loaded with a first-party admission from a covered provider that its Article 55(1)(a) evaluation instruments have ceased to be informative. Every additional day of AI Office restraint is now visible as a supervisory choice, not a procedural gap. Washington's answer this week — a Senate China-tech-loopholes package, a Robotics Commission proposal, DOE's Genesis open-weight programme, no OSTP publication of the 1 August deliverables — sketches an industrial-policy posture that treats the frontier as a competitiveness input rather than a supervisory object. California signed nothing this week but sent two bills to Newsom against a 31 August adjournment. The transatlantic policy divergence that hardened in week one has widened in week two, and the CoE Framework Convention's 1 September entry-into-force for the EU will land onto that widening gap. The evaluator-authority story is no longer external only; it is now internal, corporate, published — and, so far, unmet.
